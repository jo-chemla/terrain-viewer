Two QGIS plugins are released, both in [`qgis-plugin/`](https://github.com/Iconem/terrain-viewer/tree/main/qgis-plugin), their zips on the repository's `qgis-plugins-*` releases ([install](#install)):

- **Terrain Viewer** (0.12.0; MIT, QGIS 3.28 to 4.x): a dock panel laid out as the app's sidebar, with the app's two modes at its top since 0.10.0: Terrain, and [Historical](#the-historical-mode), dated imagery on QGIS's Temporal Controller. A backend is picked at its top: the **Python backend** (the modes computed in QGIS itself with numpy, no service at all, [QGIS Live Compute](/dev/qgis-live-compute); the default for a new user since 0.9.0), the **Packaged tile server** (a [tile service](/dev/tile-service) packaged for QGIS, downloaded and started from the panel), or a **Remote tile server** (any tile service's address). Under all three, the same modes tree: each checked mode is a layer, with its settings in the layer's address. **0.12.0 is in the repository, 0.11.0 is the released one**: what 0.12.0 adds is marked so below ([keeping a setup](#keeping-a-setup-freeze-save-to-a-folder-load), [the Vector library](#the-vector-library), [out of experimental](#experimental-until-0110)). What 0.11.0 changed (the Temporal Controller's [default step](#dates-as-steps-of-qgiss-temporal-controller), the [backend control on the Historical page](#where-the-tiles-come-from-and-the-backend-control), [Buildings from](#building-shadows-buildings-from)).
- **Send2MapApp** (0.5.1, GPL v2 or later, QGIS 3.22 to 4.x): "Open in", the place shown or clicked in QGIS opened in another viewer at the same place and scale ([below](#send2mapapp-open-in)).

Both are checked headless in QGIS 4.2 and 3.44 LTR (`qgis-plugin/test_*.py`), and Terrain Viewer's toolbar buttons and Temporal Controller button inside the application itself, offscreen (`test_app_window.py`). Neither is on plugins.qgis.org; since 0.9.0 they have a plugin repository of their own, so QGIS offers their updates ([updates](#updates)). How they came about: [design history](#design-history).

## Terrain Viewer: the panel

![The Terrain Viewer panel (0.9.1) in QGIS 4.2 over the Matterhorn: the Python backend picked (first of the three backend radios), Mapterhorn as the source, the Light fold open with its pad, Slope in color at 70% (its inline opacity slider) over Hillshade, and contour lines with their labels](/screenshots/qgis/qgis-terrain-viewer-slope-contours.jpg)

A dock panel laid out as the app's sidebar. From the top:

1. **An update**, when there is one: "Terrain Viewer 0.12.0 is available" and an **Update to 0.12.0** button ([updates](#updates)); hidden otherwise.
1. **The mode** (0.10.0): **Terrain**, everything below, or **Historical** (a clock), dated imagery on QGIS's Temporal Controller ([the Historical mode](#the-historical-mode)). Since 0.10.1 the Web toolbar has a second button, a clock, that opens the panel straight in the Historical mode.
2. **The backend**: three radio buttons in a row, as QGIS lays out a choice: **Python backend**, **Packaged tile server**, **Remote tile server** (in that order since 0.9.0), and under them the picked one's options (below).
3. **The status line**: what is connected, or why not. The backend and its status line are one control: since 0.11.0 it also shows on the Historical page while a source there needs a tile server ([below](#where-the-tiles-come-from-and-the-backend-control)).
4. **Source**, in three groups (0.9.0): **QGIS elevation layers** (the project's raster layers that can be elevation: one band of floats or 16-bit integers, "QGIS layer: ...", kept in step with the project; a photo or a hillshade picture is left out), **Worldwide defaults** (Mapterhorn, Esri, Mapbox, MapTiler, AWS, Cesium, GEM-15) and the **Terrain library** (the rest of the service's `/terrain-sources`); or a typed id, tile address, file path or GDAL source. Beside it, a **zoom to** button that moves the map canvas to the source's bounds (the whole world for a global source such as Mapterhorn), and an **add raw elevation** button: for a library COG, VRT or raw WMS, its raw elevation as a raster layer of the project, in a **Raw elevation inputs** group kept under the modes (0.9.3), opened by GDAL with the very address the Python backend reads (below).
5. **API keys**, folded behind a key icon: Mapbox, MapTiler, HERE, Google, Cesium ion. They are kept in QGIS's settings (`terrain_viewer/mapboxKey`, `maptilerKey`, `hereKey`, `googleKey`, `cesiumIonKey`). The three a tile service reads (`mapboxKey`, `maptilerKey`, `cesiumIonKey`) go into the address of every layer, so a project saved with a keyed source holds the key; the Python backend uses the Mapbox and MapTiler keys for those two sources. The Mapbox, MapTiler and HERE keys are also those basemaps' (0.9.3): with one set, [the Basemap row](#the-basemap-row) offers that basemap, and the key goes into that layer's address. Google's is kept, sent nowhere yet.
6. **Open link**: a share link, whole or just its query.
7. **Freeze**, a button with a menu on its arrow (0.12.0): **Freeze**, **Save setup to folder...**, **Save setup and GeoTIFFs to folder...**, **Load setup...** ([keeping a setup](#keeping-a-setup-freeze-save-to-a-folder-load)).
7. **Vector library**, folded (0.12.0): the app's vector overlays as QGIS layers of their own ([below](#the-vector-library)).
8. **Light**, open when the panel opens (0.9.2; folded before): the light of hillshade, Phong, terrain shadows and building shadows, one for all as in the app. Two ways to set it, radio buttons at its top:
   - **Free**: a pad (drag the point: its angle from north, clockwise, is the azimuth; its distance from the centre the elevation, 90° at the centre, 0° at the rim) and two sliders, kept in step. While the pad or a slider is dragged (0.9.0), the shaded layers follow: the first move is written at once, then a new light as soon as the canvas has drawn the last one (at most every 100 ms under Python, 200 ms with a tile service; after 2.5 s without a finished draw, anyway), and the release writes the last. 0.8.0 waited until half a second after the last move. Writing faster than the canvas draws would only cancel its renders and queue tiles nobody sees. Measured on a 1920x1080 canvas of hillshade at z13, elevation cached, the pad dragged for 4 s at 60 moves a second: the first light starts drawing at once and a new one reaches the canvas every 1.0 to 1.3 s under Python (its server computes the canvas's 40 tiles in about 0.3 s; the rest is QGIS's XYZ provider), every 2 to 2.5 s with a tile service; a smaller canvas or a zoom with fewer tiles redraws faster.
   - **Sun at a date and time** (0.8.0): a date and time picker (UTC, with a calendar), a **Day of year** slider (1 to 365; it moves the picker's date, the year kept) and an **Hour (UTC)** slider. They set the azimuth and elevation to the sun's over the map canvas's centre, which the pad and the sliders then show, with a line saying where, at what solar time and how high the sun is (below the horizon: lit from the horizon, elevation 0). Panning the canvas places the sun over the new centre (rounded to the degree, so a small pan rewrites no layer). Dragging the pad or a slider goes back to Free. The sun is the app's own model (`lib/solar-position.ts`, ported as `terrain_viewer/solar.py`: Cooper's declination, true solar time, a UTC time made solar by the longitude as the app's UTC clock); `test_panel_units.py` checks the port against the TypeScript run by Node, to 1e-6 degrees.
9. **The modes tree**: Hillshade, Elevation Hypso, Basemap (0.9.3, [below](#the-basemap-row)), then the app's groups (Terrain Analysis, Relief Visualization, Lighting Effects, Vector overlays). Since 0.9.0 Hillshade sits above Elevation Hypso, and the overlays' group reads **Vector overlays**: Contours, Iso-line, Plane slicer, Graticule, Mound candidates. Only the panel's order: the layer tree keeps the map's stacking (hillshade multiplied over the color relief) and its group stays "Overlays".

### The three backends

| Backend | What it is | When to use it | Its options |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Python backend** | The modes computed in QGIS's own Python with numpy and served from a thread of QGIS on `127.0.0.1` ([QGIS Live Compute](/dev/qgis-live-compute)) | No service at all, fast panning; every mode, from tile pyramids, COGs, VRTs, raw WMS and WCS, QGIS raster layers and anything GDAL opens. The default for a new user (0.9.0) | None: it starts when picked (0.8.0; it had a Connect button) |
| **Packaged tile server** | The tile service packaged for QGIS (Node, the service, its image libraries; 48 to 59 MB per platform), run on this machine (`127.0.0.1`) from the plugin's data folder | No desktop app, no server to run: every mode and source, nothing to set up | **Connect** (installs it the first time, then starts it; about 3 s), **Install** / **Remove** |
| **Remote tile server** | Any tile service at an address: the [desktop app's](#with-the-desktop-app) (`http://localhost:3400`), one you run (`cd tile-server && pnpm start`, e.g. `http://localhost:3400`), or a hosted one | The desktop app is open, you develop the service, or a team shares one | The address, **Connect** |

- **Connect** works the same for both tile servers: the service is asked `/health` first (3 s), so a service that is not there gives one message in the status line and the message bar, and no layer is made until a backend is connected (no tile asks a dead address). Picking another backend connects to it (the packaged tile server once installed: its download waits for Connect; the Python backend at once): the checked modes' layers are re-pointed to it (their style, opacity and resampling kept), and the local service, if it ran, stops.
- The panel opening (the toolbar button, a saved project) connects a remote tile server or starts the Python backend (a thread of QGIS, nothing to download), but never starts the packaged tile server: it waits for **Connect**.
- 0.6.0's bug, "with Local tile service checked, Connect does nothing": the button's `clicked(checked=False)` argument reached `connect_service(start_bundled)` as "do not start". Every button now drops it.
- While connected to a service, its `/health` is asked every 15 s in the background; a service that went away is said once, and its return too.
- **Which one a new user gets** (0.9.0): the Python backend, which needs nothing (no 50 MB download, no service to run) and draws every mode; the packaged tile server stays one click away. A user who picked a backend keeps it, and so does a user of 0.6.0 or earlier (the "Local tile service" box, or a service address typed). Without numpy in QGIS's Python, the remote tile server.

![Sky View Factor under the Python backend, its settings cog open: color ramp, Min (raised to 50) and Max, Invert color ramp, Radius and Precision](/screenshots/qgis/qgis-terrain-viewer-svf-settings.jpg)

### One modes tree for every backend

The tree comes from the service's `/processes` (each mode as an [OGC API - Processes](/dev/tile-service#visualization-modes-ogc-api---processes) description: its group, form, shading, opacity parameter, settings with their types and defaults). Before any service answers, and for the Python backend, it comes from the snapshot the plugin carries (`assets/catalog.json`, made by `qgis-plugin/make_assets.py` from a tile service), so the tree is there at once and the same under every backend. A mode or parameter added to the app reaches the panel through the service with no plugin release.

- **A mode's row**: its checkbox on the left, its forms on the right, then the settings cog. A click anywhere on the row's line toggles the mode, not only on the box. The forms are **Color** (the mode as the app draws it, an XYZ layer), **Values** (the numbers before the color ramp, a Float32 raster GDAL reads from the service's `/gdal.xml`, for the raster calculator, zonal statistics or Identify; only for the modes computed as numbers) and, for the overlays, **Vector** then **Raster**.
- **Hover** (0.8.0): a pointing hand over what takes a click (a mode's line, its boxes and cog, the group titles, the folds, the buttons, the backends), and a light background under a mode's line.
- **The first layer and the canvas**: in a fresh, empty project (QGIS just started, or **Project > New**), the panel's first layer sets the project up as QGIS would: the project in EPSG:3857 (the tiles' CRS), the canvas on the whole world (0.9.0; 0.8.0 left the canvas where it was even then). After that, with every mode unchecked and no other layer, the next mode checked keeps the canvas where it is (0.8.0): QGIS's own first-layer setup (`QgsLayerTreeMapCanvasBridge.setAutoSetupOnFirstLayer`) is off until QGIS has seen the panel's layer, then back on. A project opened from a file is never set up again.
- **Overlays default to vector tiles**: checking contours, iso-line, mound candidates, plane slicer or graticule turns on its Vector form (sharp at every zoom, styled in QGIS in the mode's color); Raster draws it as the app does. Contours are black by default (0.9.2), lines and vector tiles, under every backend.
- **Labels**: the contours' major lines carry their elevation as QGIS labels, on by default in the layer's style (Layer Properties > Labels changes them), black text in a thin white buffer, placed by QGIS along the lines (curved), for the tile service's vector tiles and the Python backend's lines alike. 0.9.0 halved them (4.25 pt text in a 0.5 mm buffer; 0.8.0's 8.5 pt in 1 mm were at least twice too large) and kept them on the contours only: the iso-line, plane slicer and graticule have none. No label points: the vector tile style draws a point symbol only for the mound detector, whose points are its features.
- Checking a mode turns on its default form (the first time; afterwards the forms it had); each form can be checked alone or with the others, and checking one checks the mode. Unchecking the mode or its last form **removes** its layers, every layer with its key (a copy made with QGIS's Duplicate Layer carries the key: 0.6.0 left it drawing), and **the groups left empty go too**, the live group itself when nothing is left. A layer removed by hand unchecks its form.
- **Under the Python backend**, the modes it does not compute are greyed out, with a note on hover; the few settings it ignores (an iso-line's cliff edge, Phong's and matcap's raster renderers) are greyed out too; the color ramps, ranges, inversion, hillshade methods and colors, Phong's lights and the rest apply as in the tile service ([parity](/dev/qgis-live-compute#parity-with-the-tile-service)); and only the forms it makes show, Values only where the mode has values (hillshade is color only).
- **Basemap** (0.9.3): see [the Basemap row](#the-basemap-row).
- **Opacity**, in the row (0.9.0; it was under the cog): a short slider and its percentage between the mode's name and its forms, shown while a drawn form (Color, Vector) is on. It is the QGIS layer's opacity.
- **Settings**, under the cog while a drawn form is on: every number that has a usual range is a **slider with its number box**, as in the app (sun azimuth and elevation, opacities, exaggeration, radii, ranges; the box takes values beyond the slider). **Color ramps** are a select with each ramp's preview, **matcap textures** a select with each texture's preview (the app's 20), both packaged with the plugin as sprite sheets (`assets/colorramps.png`, `assets/matcaps.png`, made by `make_assets.py` from the service's `/colorramps/{id}.png` and the app's matcap pictures). Colors are QGIS color buttons.
- **Symmetric ramps** (LRM, TPI, curvature, openness, local dominance, shape index, and the hypsometric tint's "symmetric"): checked, the maximum is edited and the minimum follows it live (minimum = -maximum), read only.
- A changed setting rewrites the layer's address half a second after the last change (`setDataSource`, which keeps the renderer, opacity, blending and resampling set in the layer's properties). Only the settings that differ from the app's defaults go into it.
- **Opacity and blending are QGIS layer settings**: the tiles are asked opaque (QGIS draws neighbouring tiles a pixel over each other, so half transparent tiles show lines along their edges); the shading modes (hillshade, Phong, terrain and building shadows) are multiplied over the layers under them.
- Each form is a layer of the "Terrain Viewer (live)" group, in a subgroup named as the app's panel group, stacked as the map stacks them; the Python backend's layers go in the same tree (0.6.0 had a separate "local compute" group, removed on connect).

### Building shadows: Buildings from

0.11.0. Until 0.10.1 the building shadows read OpenFreeMap's OpenStreetMap buildings only, under every backend. Under the Building shadows row's cog, **Buildings from** now picks where the footprints and the heights come from, as the app's shadows do since they read any vector source with heights (`buildingShadowSource`):

| Buildings from | What it is | What to give |
|---|---|---|
| **Library (vector sources with heights)** | The app's Library entries that have a height field: OpenStreetMap buildings from OpenFreeMap (the default), Overture Maps buildings (a PMTiles archive per monthly release, its parts and floor counts read), and the others | Nothing: the entry names its layers and fields. The list is the plugin's own (`assets/app_defaults.json`, made by `make_app_defaults.py` from `lib/custom-sources.json`), with what a connected tile service lists at `/building-sources` added |
| **Custom address...** | A PMTiles archive (`.pmtiles`), a TileJSON, or a `{z}/{x}/{y}` vector tile template of your own | **Address**, **Layer** (the vector tile layer holding the polygons, several comma-separated; empty: every layer the archive or the TileJSON lists, `building` for a template) and **Height field** (metres; `height` when empty) |
| **QGIS layer: ...** | Any polygon layer of the project, whatever its provider and CRS | **Height field** (its numeric and text fields, the likely one picked: `height`, `hauteur`, `render_height`...; or **One height for all**), **Metres per unit** (1; 3.2 for a field of floors, 0.3048 for feet) and **Height without one** (for a building whose field is empty; 0 leaves it out) |

How to, with a layer of your own:

1. Add the polygon layer to the project (a GeoPackage, a shapefile, a PostGIS table, a WFS: anything QGIS reads as polygons).
2. Check **Building shadows** (Lighting Effects), open its cog, pick **QGIS layer: your layer** under **Buildings from**.
3. Pick the **Height field**; set **Metres per unit** when the field is not metres, and **Height without one** when some buildings have none. The line under it says how many buildings were read.
4. Set the sun in **Light** (free, or the sun at a date and time). After editing the layer, the refresh button beside the choice reads it again.

How a QGIS layer reaches the backends: the panel writes its polygons to a GeoJSON in the profile (`<profile>/terrain_viewer/buildings/<layer id>.geojson`: longitude and latitude, each building's height in `height`), on the main thread, once per choice.

- **Python backend**: the file is read with OGR, reprojected to Web Mercator and burnt into each tile's height grid, exactly as a vector tile's buildings are (`live/buildings.py`), then the flat-ground shadow sweep.
- **A tile server on this machine** (the packaged one, the desktop app's, one started by hand): the file is registered with it (`POST /files`) and the layer's address carries `buildingShadowSource=file:<id>` ([tile service](/dev/tile-service#building-shadows-where-the-buildings-come-from)); never a path. A tile server on another machine cannot read the file: said once, OpenFreeMap's buildings drawn; use the Python backend for it.
- **A layer of at most 50 000 features is written whole.** A larger one is written for the map's extent and three kilometres around (a shadow's longest reach), 200 000 features at most, and written again when the map leaves that extent.
- **A tile server that does not answer `/building-sources`** (one from before this change: the bundles pinned by 0.10.1's release among them) reads OpenFreeMap's buildings only: any other choice is said once and drawn with the default until the service is updated. The bundles 0.11.0 pins read them.

Both backends from the same buildings give the same picture: measured on one z16 tile of Courbevoie (`test_buildings_live.py`, 2026-11-26, QGIS 4.2 and 3.44), a six-building layer in Lambert-93 swept by the tile service and burnt by the Python backend agree on 96 % of the shaded pixels (the rest is the rasteriser's edge), OpenFreeMap's buildings on 99 %, Overture's on 98 %. Timings there, nothing cached: OpenFreeMap 1.2 s for the first tile under Python, Overture 6 to 8 s (its archive's header and directories, then about 1 MB a tile; a quarter of that for the tiles after).

- **PMTiles in plain Python** (`live/pmtiles.py`, no dependency): the archive's header and directories once, then one range request a tile; gzip or uncompressed archives (brotli and zstd are refused with a message). Checked against an archive written by the test and against the `pmtiles` package's tile numbering.
- **Heights**: a feature's height field, else its floors times 3.2 m, else 4 m, as the app (a QGIS layer: the field times **Metres per unit**, else **Height without one**). A raised part's base is read by a tile server (it shades from its base); the Python backend burns it from the ground.
- **The choice is kept in the project** (`terrain_viewer/buildingsFrom`), and **Open link** reads a link's `buildingShadowSource` (an id or an address) into it.

### Any GDAL source

**Under the Python backend**, the elevation is whatever the source picker names, read in Python:

- **Tile pyramids**, decoded in numpy: Terrarium and Terrain-RGB (Mapterhorn, the default; AWS Terrain; the library's other public ones; Mapbox and MapTiler with their keys; a typed `{z}/{x}/{y}` address).
- **Anything GDAL opens**: a QGIS raster layer of the project (its file through GDAL; a WMS or WCS layer of QGIS through its own provider), a local GeoTIFF, COG or VRT, a `/vsicurl/` address, an https GeoTIFF or VRT, a `WCS:` string or a GDAL WMS description (`<GDAL_WMS>...`). Each tile is a window of a `gdal.Warp` to a VRT on the zoom's own Web Mercator grid (overviews picked by GDAL), no data as NaN, scale and offset applied, feet turned into metres, then the same kernels ([details and timings](/dev/qgis-live-compute#any-gdal-raster-as-the-source)).
- **The library's raw elevation** (0.9.0), by the `address` the service's `/terrain-sources` lists (and the packaged snapshot): a **COG or VRT** over `/vsicurl/` (Canada's NRCan MRDEM, one 84 GB COG in EPSG:3979, its overviews read by GDAL; Switzerland's swissALTIRegio, the Nepal and Iceland strips, ...), a **raw WMS** (`SERVICE=WMS&REQUEST=GetMap`, a Float32 GeoTIFF format: France's IGN LiDAR HD DSM and DTM, RGE ALTI, the US 3DEP WMS) as a GDAL WMS description (WMS minidriver, a data window over the entry's bounds at its finest resolution in EPSG:3857, one Float32 band, empty blocks for 404 and a service exception, GDAL's overviews for a zoomed-out view), and the **other raw services** (WCS 1.0 and 2.0 GetCoverage, ArcGIS exportImage: the Netherlands' AHN, Flanders, NRW, Norway, the US 3DEP image service, ...) asked as the app asks them, one bounding box per tile with its pixel of margin, each GeoTIFF answer decoded and warped onto the tile by GDAL. The library's `nodataFloor` turns a service's own no data into holes.
- **Not read**: LERC (Esri), quantized mesh (Cesium), PMTiles and the differences of two sources. Picking one says so once and **draws nothing** (0.9.0): 0.8.0 drew Mapterhorn in its place, so a source it refused showed the whole world's hillshade instead (the user picked NRCan's COG and saw that).

How to use a raster of your own as the elevation:

1. **A file or a service QGIS opens**: add it to the project (Layer > Add Raster Layer, or drag it in); it is listed under **QGIS elevation layers** as `QGIS layer: <name>`; pick it.
2. **A COG or VRT on the web**: type its address (`https://.../dem.tif`, or `/vsicurl/https://...`) in the source box and press Enter.
3. **A WMS or WCS GDAL describes**: type its GDAL string (`WCS:https://...`, `<GDAL_WMS>...</GDAL_WMS>`), or add it as a QGIS layer and pick that.
4. **A library COG, VRT or raw WMS, seen as raw values**: pick it, press the **add raw elevation** button beside the source: the same GDAL address becomes a layer in **Raw elevation inputs** (Identify reads its metres, QGIS styles it), and the Python backend reads it the same way whether it is picked by its library name or as that layer. Since 0.9.3 the group stays right under **Terrain Viewer (live)**, and the layer's grey is stretched to what the canvas shows ([what is read](#what-the-python-backend-reads-and-at-what-resolution)).

Why a button, not a layer added on every pick: a raw input layer is drawn by QGIS like any other, so every pan asks its service again (an IGN WMS, an 84 GB COG); added only when asked, it costs nothing otherwise, and the Python backend never needs it (it reads the address itself).

### What the Python backend reads, and at what resolution

Two things, said plainly:

- **The raw elevations, whatever the layer's symbology.** A QGIS raster layer picked as the source is never read through its renderer. For a layer of GDAL's provider (a file, a COG, a VRT, a raw input) the plugin takes the layer's file address (`layer.source()`) and opens it with GDAL itself, band 1: the color ramp, the hillshade renderer, the stretch, the opacity, the blending and the resampling set in Layer Properties play no part, and neither does a "no data" value added there (only the file's own no data, scale and offset are applied, and feet are turned into metres). For a layer of another provider (WMS, WCS, ArcGIS) it makes a provider of its own from the layer's address and asks it for blocks of band 1 (`QgsRasterProjector` over the data provider, not the layer's render pipe): data values again, not the picture. `terrain_viewer/live/gdal_source.py` (`layer_spec`, `GdalDem`, `QgsProviderDem`); `test_panel_units.py` reads the same tile of a GeoTIFF under a hillshade renderer, a color ramp and a grey stretch at 30% opacity: the same metres each time. One consequence: a layer QGIS draws as a hillshade because its file *is* a hillshade picture (bytes 0 to 255) is not elevation, and is not listed under **QGIS elevation layers**.
- **A window per tile, never the whole file.** Every mode is computed tile by tile, on 256 px Web Mercator tiles at the zoom QGIS asks for (the canvas's resolution, rounded to the nearest tile zoom by QGIS's XYZ provider; the Values form reads the level just finer), each with a one tile ring around it for the kernels' halo: a 1600 x 900 canvas is 28 to 40 tiles. What is read for one tile is a 256 x 256 window of the source at that zoom's resolution: the table below says from where.

| Source | What one tile reads |
|---|---|
| **COG** (`/vsicurl/`) | A 256 x 256 window of a GDAL warped VRT of that zoom (the raster in EPSG:3857 on the zoom's pixel grid, bilinear, whatever its own CRS). GDAL picks the overview nearest that resolution and fetches only the blocks the window covers, by HTTP range requests. Past the COG's own resolution, the same windows are interpolated |
| **VRT** (`/vsicurl/`) | The same warped window. GDAL opens the sources the window touches and reads their overviews; a VRT of many files with no overview of its own is slow zoomed out (every file under the canvas is opened), fast zoomed in |
| **QGIS raster layer**, GDAL provider (a file, a COG, a VRT, a raw input) | Its file through the same GDAL path, so the same windows and overviews. Its style in QGIS plays no part |
| **QGIS raster layer**, another provider (WMS, WCS, ArcGIS) | One 256 x 256 block per tile asked from the provider over the tile's extent, reprojected by QGIS |
| **Raw WMS** of the library | A GDAL WMS dataset: the service is asked for the blocks under the window, at the overview level of the zoom |
| **WCS, ArcGIS exportImage** | One bounding box request per source tile, at the tile's own size |

Measured on Canada's NRCan MRDEM (one 84 GB COG in EPSG:3979, 185220 x 166668 px, 9 overviews; `test_sources.py`): a hillshade tile of western Canada at z3 warps 9 windows and downloads 6 MB, a tile of Banff at z12 (the COG's own resolution) 22 MB with the file's header, and a whole 1600 x 900 canvas draws in 2 to 10 s the first time (then from the cache).

A tile service reads the same way, tile by tile, in its own reader.

**A hillshade that showed nothing over a raw input (fixed in 0.9.3).** The tiles were computed; they were hidden. A raw input is an opaque raster, and 0.9.2 put **Raw elevation inputs** at the top of the layer tree: with a **Terrain Viewer (live)** group there already (any mode or the basemap checked before), the raw layer covered every mode. With the modes on top, the hillshade multiplies over the raw layer, which QGIS stretched once to the whole raster's range (0 to 5900 m for Canada): black lowlands, and a hillshade multiplied over black stays black. Now the group is kept right under the live group (moved there when a mode's layer joins the tree), and the raw layer's grey is stretched to the 2nd to 98th percentile of what the canvas shows, again at every view. To see a shaded mode alone, uncheck the raw layer.

### The Basemap row

A row of the modes tree, under Elevation Hypso (the app's stack: the basemap at the bottom, [layer order](/dev/layer-order)), for every backend:

- **Its checkbox** adds or removes one XYZ layer, at the bottom of **Terrain Viewer (live)**: under every mode and group, whatever is checked later. Removing the layer by hand unchecks the row.
- **Its opacity**, in the row as for the modes.
- **Source**, under the cog: the app's built-in basemaps (`lib/basemap-sources.ts`, carried by `assets/app_defaults.json`, which `make_app_defaults.py` writes): Google Hybrid, Bing Aerial, ESRI World Imagery (the default, as in the app), Google Satellite, and Mapbox Satellite, MapTiler Satellite and HERE Satellite once their key is set under **API keys** (a keyed basemap whose key the panel does not hold is not offered). Picking another re-points the same layer and renames it.
- **Open link** drives the same row (`showRasterBasemap`, `basemapSourceA` or `basemapSource`, `rasterBasemapOpacity`). A link's basemap that is none of the choices is added to the select: an XYZ address (`basemapUrl`), or a dated source (Wayback, Planet, ...), which a tile service draws through its `/basemap-tiles` with the link's date; under the Python backend ESRI World Imagery stands in for it, said once.
- A built-in basemap is read from its own address, with no backend in between; a saved project's basemap layer is read back into the row.


### The Historical mode

The app's [historical imagery mode](/features/historical-imagery) (`appMode=historical`), 0.10.0, extended in 0.10.1. The switch at the top of the panel, **Terrain** and **Historical** (a clock), shows one page or the other; the choice is kept (`terrain_viewer/appMode`), and the Web toolbar's second button, a clock (0.10.1), opens the panel straight in it. The layers of either mode stay in the project when the other is shown, so dated imagery under a hillshade is one picture.

- **Imagery**, in two groups. **Historical sources** (below). **Worldwide defaults (no dates)**: the app's built-in basemaps, which [the Basemap row](#the-basemap-row) draws. One basemap at a time: a historical source unchecks the Basemap row, a worldwide default removes the dated layers. An opacity beside the select.
- **The dates** at the map's centre: the current one between **previous** and **next**, with who took it, its resolution and the release it came from; a time axis with one tick per date, each at its place in time (a click picks the nearest); and the list, newest first.
- **Open link** reads a link in the app's historical mode: `appMode=historical` switches the mode, moves the canvas, picks view A's source (`historicalActiveSourceA`) and the date nearest `dateA`. A source that cannot be used now (no key, no tile server) is said with its reason, and Esri Wayback takes its place.

| Source | Its dates | Its tiles | Needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Esri World Imagery Wayback** | The real capture dates at the map's centre, one per distinct capture (every archived release since 2014) | Each release's own XYZ address, to z19 | Nothing |
| **Google Earth historical** (0.10.1) | Every dated capture Google Earth holds at the centre (at the Eiffel Tower: 48, from 1943 to 2026), each with its provider | A tile service's `/basemap-tiles/ge-historical`, to z21 | A tile server backend connected, from 2026-11-26 or later: picked and connected on this page since 0.11.0 |
| **Planet monthly**, **Planet quarterly** (0.10.1) | Every mosaic, computed as the app computes them: monthly from 2020-01 to two months back, quarterly from 2016 Q1 | Planet's XYZ address with the key, to z15 | A Planet key |
| **NASA HLS** (0.10.1) | A monthly cadence from 2016 (about 130 dates): not captures found at the place | titiler-cmr's XYZ address (NASA's public demonstration server: slow, sometimes down), to z16 | Nothing |
| **EOX Sentinel-2 cloudless** | One 10 m mosaic a year, 2016 to 2025 | EOX's XYZ address, to z14 | Nothing |

A source that waits for a key is greyed out in the select with its reason ("needs a Planet key"), and offered as soon as the key is typed. Google Earth historical says "needs a tile server backend" until one is connected, and since 0.11.0 it can be picked anyway: picking it brings the backend control to the page.

#### Where the tiles come from, and the backend control

Esri Wayback, Planet, NASA HLS and EOX are **the providers' own XYZ tiles, read by QGIS itself**. Nothing stands in between: not the Python backend, not a tile server. They work with any backend connected or with none, and a line under the select says so: "Esri Wayback needs no backend: its tiles are the provider's own XYZ address, read by QGIS itself".

**The backend control on this page (0.11.0).** In 0.10.1, Google Earth historical needed a tile server connected from the Terrain page: a detour, the source being picked here. Now, while a source that needs a tile server is picked, the Historical page shows the panel's backend control under the imagery select: the three radio buttons, the picked one's options (**Connect**, **Install**, the address) and the status line.

- **It is the Terrain page's own control, moved, not a second one** (`place_backend_box` in `dock.py` puts the one widget in the page that needs it): one setting (`terrain_viewer/backend`), one connection, one tile server. A backend picked here is the Terrain mode's backend too, and its checked modes' layers follow it, as when picked there.
- **How to**: pick **Google Earth historical**, then **Packaged tile server** (Connect installs it the first time and starts it) or **Remote tile server** (its address, Connect). The dates list as soon as it answers. The Python backend does not read Google Earth, and the line says so.
- **A source that needs none hides it**: back on Esri Wayback, the control returns to the Terrain page.
- **Esri Wayback is not routed through a tile server**, though a tile service lists its dates too (`/imagery-dates/wayback`). Measured at the Eiffel Tower and in Lyon (2026-11-26, nothing cached): 12 s through the service against 15 to 29 s in the plugin, both waiting on Esri's own metadata service. But the service answers the whole list at once (the plugin shows the first date after 5 s and fills in), it names dates without the releases whose XYZ tiles QGIS would read (its Wayback tiles are drawn by the browser engine, which the packaged tile server does not have), and the plugin keeps what it found between sessions (a place seen before: under a second). A server in between would add a requirement and take away the dates arriving one by one, for a gain on the first visit of a place only.

Google Earth historical is the exception. Its tiles are not Web Mercator (a plate carrée quadtree of encrypted images), so they cannot be an XYZ address QGIS asks. A [tile service](/dev/tile-service#google-earth-historical-dates-and-web-mercator-tiles-no-browser) lists the dates of a place (`/imagery-dates/ge-historical`) and builds plain Web Mercator tiles of a date (`/basemap-tiles/ge-historical/{z}/{x}/{y}.jpg?imageryDate=...`), in Node, with no browser engine, so the packaged tile server does it too. The plugin uses both when a tile server backend is connected (**Packaged tile server** or **Remote tile server**, picked and connected on this page or on the Terrain page: the same control), and the line under the select names its address; without one the line says what to pick. The dates are asked at the Keyhole level of the tiles QGIS draws (the app's zoom plus one). An older service (before 2026-11-26) answers 404 and the panel says to update it: the bundles pinned by 0.9.3's release are older, so the packaged tile server lists Google Earth's dates from the next plugin release on. The Python backend does not read Google Earth yet.

#### Planet: the key

**API keys**, folded at the bottom of the Historical page as in the Terrain mode, holds the Planet key (`terrain_viewer/planetKey` in QGIS's settings, a password field). It goes into the address of each Planet layer and nowhere else: it is not one of the Terrain mode's keys, so it is never sent to a tile service, and no status line, warning or log of the panel shows it (an error that quotes an address has its key taken out). A project saved with Planet layers holds the key in their addresses, as for the other keyed basemaps. Every tile counts against the key's quota: one layer per month means QGIS only asks the tiles of the month drawn. Once a session the key is tried on one world tile of the newest mosaic, so a key Planet refuses (HTTP 401 or 403) is said in the panel instead of 80 layers of blank tiles. The mosaics are computed, not listed, as in the app (`lib/planet.ts`).

#### Listing Esri Wayback's dates, and what the panel says meanwhile

Esri answers slowly, so the listing runs in a worker thread (`urllib`, as the Python backend reads its tiles), the panel reading its progress, and QGIS stays free:

1. **The catalog**, one request: some 200 releases.
2. **The walk** (`tilemap`, about 25 requests in eight chains started across the catalog): the 10 to 30 releases whose imagery changed at the centre's tile. This is the app's own shortcut (`lib/wayback.ts`): the other releases repeat their neighbour's picture there, and are never asked for a date.
3. **One metadata query per kept release**, six at a time, for its capture date (Esri's metadata service answers in 1 to 30 s). Several releases in a row often carry the same capture: one date per capture, the newest release of it. A release whose date does not answer (asked twice) stands at its release date, marked.

What 0.10.1 changed:

- **The dates are added as they answer** to the list, the axis and the layer group, with a thin progress bar and "3 of 14 releases dated", also after a pan (0.10.0 waited for the whole list then). Only dated releases appear: a release date is not a capture date. The layers on screen stay until the new list is whole, so the imagery does not go and come back.
- **The releases that can hold the date asked for are dated first** (imagery is never newer than its release: the first release at or after the date, then outward), the newest first when no date is asked.
- **What was found is kept between sessions**, in `<profile>/terrain_viewer/wayback-dates.json` (the last 300 places): a walk until a new release comes out, a capture date for good. A place seen before costs one request.
- **A notice above the dates** says what the dates shown are worth: *The map moved: these dates are of the previous place. Updating (3 of 14 releases dated)...* from the moment the centre leaves the tile (a quarter of a tile, as the app) until the new list is whole; *Dates still arriving* during a first listing; and, zoomed out, *Zoom in to see the capture dates. Esri Wayback dates a place from zoom 10 on (about 1:250 000 here); the map is at zoom 7.3. Until then only the newest release is listed: a wider view has no one date.* With **Follow the map** off the dates are not listed again, and the notice says how to (the list button).

Measured at the Eiffel Tower (2026-11-26, QGIS 4.2 and 3.44): the first date after 5 s, all nine after 25 s, 40 requests; listed again in another session, 0.9 s.

#### Dates as steps of QGIS's Temporal Controller

Every date is **one XYZ raster layer** of the group **Historical imagery**, at the bottom of **Terrain Viewer (live)**, named with its true date (`2018-09-11 · Esri Wayback`), with a **fixed temporal range** (`QgsRasterLayerTemporalProperties`, `FixedTemporalRange`): **from its true capture date until the next capture's**; a mosaic's range is its month, quarter or year (HLS: the 30 days its mosaic is made of). The ranges are half open, so no two overlap, and the newest stays drawn after its own. So at any time of the controller, the layer whose range holds that time is the latest capture at or before it. A source of months is 80 to 130 layers: its group starts folded.

The panel then sets the canvas's temporal navigation (`canvas.temporalController()`, the `QgsTemporalNavigationObject` behind **View > Panels > Temporal Controller**) to animated navigation, with those ranges as its available ranges. The **Temporal Controller** button shows QGIS's panel, and **step**, beside it, picks how its slider divides time. **Since 0.11.0 (in the repository) the default is a regular step, one week**; 0.10.1's default was source timestamps, which read as a fixed step that ignored the dates: its stops are evenly spaced whatever the time between two captures. The choice is kept in `terrain_viewer/temporalStep` (0.10.1 kept it in `historicalStep`, which is no longer read, so every user starts on the week once).

| Step | The slider | The controller's date | What a frame draws |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Day** | Proportional to time: 5600 frames for 14 years | The day | The latest capture at or before that day, exactly |
| **Week** (the default) | Proportional to time: 800 frames for 14 years, each from a Monday | The week | The latest capture at or before the end of the week; two captures of one week share a frame, the newer drawn |
| **Month** | Proportional to time: 187 frames for 14 years, calendar months | The month | The newest capture begun by the end of the month |
| **Year** | 16 frames for 14 years | The year | The newest capture of the year |
| **Source timestamps** | One stop per date, nothing in between (`IrregularStep`): nine stops at the Eiffel Tower, evenly spaced whatever the time between them | The capture's own: each frame's range begins on the capture date | That capture: every stop changes the picture |

Under a regular step most frames change nothing: time passes between captures, which is what a slider proportional to time shows. A line under the dates says where the controller is and what it draws (0.11.0): *Controller: week of 2019-06-03. Imagery: captured 2018-09-11, the latest at or before it.*, or *..., in this frame.* when the capture falls in the frame. The date in bold above, the list's row and the layer drawn are always the capture's true date.

How a regular step works, and its limits, as measured headless in QGIS 4.2 and 3.44:

- The frames have one duration, from the start of the first date's day, week, month or year, so **the slider's position is the date's place in time** (checked to a frame for every date).
- **Each frame draws the capture current at that time**: QGIS draws the layers whose range meets the frame, so a frame between two captures shows the older one until the newer begins. The controller's date is then the frame's, not the capture's: at June 2019 the Eiffel Tower shows the capture of 2018-09-11, and the panel says 2018-09-11. That is the trade: the date the controller shows is exact, and the imagery is the latest as of it.
- **A date picked in the panel** moves the slider to the frame that holds it. When a newer capture begins in the same frame (two captures in one month, under the month step), QGIS draws both and the newer is on top: the panel shows the newer and says that a finer step shows the other. The panel always shows the date drawn.
- **Why one week is the default (0.11.0)**: the slider's position is the date, an animation's speed is real time, and a week is short enough that two captures rarely share a frame (at the Eiffel Tower, none of Esri Wayback's dates does; of Google Earth's 48 since 1943, two weeks hold two captures, where five months do). A day is exact and long; a month is a short slider; source timestamps is the one step where every stop changes the picture, for stepping from capture to capture.
- **Checked for real** (`test_temporal_live.py`, 2026-11-26, QGIS 4.2 and 3.44): at the Eiffel Tower under the week step, at 47 frames of Esri Wayback's 800 and 68 of Google Earth's 4327, the panel names the latest capture at or before the frame's end and the layer QGIS draws on top is that capture's, named with its date; the rendered picture changes at the frame a capture begins in (all 256 sampled pixels) and not three weeks on (none).
- QGIS counts a month as 30 days when it numbers the frames of a regular step, so a slider of months ends a frame or two past the last date; the newest layer's range is left open, so those frames draw it.

Common to every step:

- Stepping, dragging or playing the controller draws the layer of the frame. The panel follows (it listens to `updateTemporalRange`), and a date picked in the panel moves the slider.
- The group works without the plugin: a saved project reopens with its dates, and the controller's animation export writes one picture per frame.
- **One layer per date**: only the one of the frame is drawn. With the controller turned off (its first button), QGIS would draw all of them: the panel then checks the current date's layer alone, and a pick swaps the checked layer.
- **One navigation for the project.** The available ranges are the panel's; a project with other temporal layers gets its own ranges back by picking **source timestamps** again in the controller.
- Removing several XYZ layers while they draw holds QGIS's event loop on their tile requests (five layers mid-draw: minutes, headless). The panel stops the canvas's rendering before it removes dated layers.
- A Python slot on the navigation's `navigationModeChanged` signal crashes QGIS 3.44 (Qt 5) when the mode changes; the panel listens to `updateTemporalRange` alone, which a change of mode emits too.
- **Remove** takes the group out and lists nothing again until the list button, another source or a link.

**The Temporal Controller button that did nothing (0.10.0).** It looked for QGIS's panel by the type of the widget inside it (`findChild(QgsTemporalControllerWidget)` on each dock). In the application that finds nothing: the widget is not reachable by its type from Python, so the button only wrote a hint in the status line. 0.10.1 finds the dock by its object name, `Temporal Controller` (the same in QGIS 3.44 and 4.2; the title is translated, the object name is not), and shows it through QGIS's own checkable action `mActionTemporalController` (View > Panels, the toolbar's clock), then `setUserVisible(true)`, which also raises it when it is tabbed behind another panel. `qgis-plugin/test_app_window.py` runs this inside the application (QGIS 4.2 and 3.44, offscreen, a throwaway profile): hidden before the click, visible after, the action checked.

**The crash at exit on QGIS 3.44 (found in 0.11.0).** A headless script that drew building shadows with the Python backend ended with a segmentation fault (exit code 139) after its last line, every check passed. The cause: an OGR in-memory dataset (a tile's footprints, kept in the backend's cache) still held by Python when `QgsApplication.exitQgis()` tears GDAL down; Python then frees it into a GDAL that is gone. Reproduced with one such dataset alone on 3.44 (not on 4.2), gone once the cache is dropped first: the live server's shutdown now drops it (`live/buildings.py` `clear`), which the plugin's unload calls too.

**Not yet**: Bing (one current mosaic: it is a Basemap source), the national series and the STAC catalogs, source coverage, a second view to compare two dates, Google Earth historical under the Python backend.

### Local files for the tile server

**A QGIS raster layer with a tile service**: a service on this machine (the packaged one, the desktop app's, one started with `pnpm start`) cannot be given a path in a tile address, so the panel hands it the layer's GeoTIFF or VRT file once (`POST /files`, [local files](/dev/tile-service#local-files)) and every address then names it as `terrainSource=file:<id>`, never by its path (`terrain_viewer/local_files.py`). The id is an HMAC of the real path, size and modification time (a key in the service's cache folder): not guessable, the same while the file is unchanged, new when it changes. A service on another machine cannot read this machine's files: an https COG or VRT goes to it as its address, anything else is for the Python backend (said once).

How to:

1. Pick **Packaged tile server** (or **Remote tile server** with `http://localhost:3400`, the desktop app's), **Connect**.
2. Add the GeoTIFF or VRT to the project and pick it under **QGIS elevation layers**: the panel registers it, the status line says so, the layers draw from it.
3. On a shared or exposed service, the operator limits what can be registered: `TV_FILE_ROOTS` (folders the file, and every local file of a VRT, must lie under; `;`-separated on Windows, `:` elsewhere), or `TV_LOCAL_FILES=off` (no registration at all; the plugin then says the file is for the Python backend). Registration is accepted only from the same machine, without an `Origin` header (no web page can register a file).

### Open link, Freeze, saved projects

- **Open link**: paste a share link (`https://terrain-viewer.iconem.com/?zoom=...`, any other address such as the desktop app's internal `views://app/index.html?...`, or none) or only its query (`zoom=12.34&lat=...`, with or without the `?`); its modes are checked, their settings and opacities set, the terrain source picked, the light set, the canvas moved to its camera (as an extent in the project's CRS, which is kept). The link is read as the app reads it (0.9.2): a parameter it leaves out takes the app's default, so `showTerrainAnalysis=true` alone draws the slope (`showSlope` defaults to true), `showReliefVisualization=true` the local relief model, and the hillshade is on unless `showHillshade=false`; the settings and the light it leaves out go back to their defaults; a mode's opacity is its own times its group's (`slopeOpacity` x `terrainAnalysisOpacity`). With a service, `/view?<the link's query>` answers what the view switches on (the reading `/qgis.qgz` does); under Python, the panel reads the switches over the app's defaults (`assets/app_defaults.json`, made by `make_app_defaults.py` from the app's URL parameters and built-in basemaps). With `showRasterBasemap=true`, the link checks [the Basemap row](#the-basemap-row) with the view's basemap (`basemapSourceA`, or `basemapSource` with `basemapPerView=false`; ESRI World Imagery by default) at `rasterBasemapOpacity`; a link without it unchecks the row. Since 0.9.3 the row is the one way a basemap layer is made. `lightUseDatetime=true` sets the light to the sun at the link's day and time. The service is asked first: if it is not there, one message, no layer.
- The link's source is kept: `terrainSourceA` (the app's name for view A's source) is read as the source, and an id the list lacks is added to it and picked (0.6.0 typed it into the box, where it was ignored, so the previous source, for instance `custom-at-tirol-dgm5`, stayed in every address).
- The link's other parameters go into every layer's address only when some mode takes them (`/processes`' inputs): the app's own, such as `splitRatio`, the camera or view B's source, stay out of the tiles (0.6.0 forwarded them all).
- **Freeze** copies the layers drawn now (the checked ones) into a new group under the live one, which stays on top, named with the date, the time and the place ("Frozen 2026-11-24 14:05 — 44.51, 4.00 z11"): XYZ, vector tile, values and the Python backend's layers alike, every setting in their addresses, with their opacity, blending, style and order. The copies carry no mark the panel looks for, so later changes leave them as they are; several freezes stack, the newest on top. Since 0.12.0 each copy carries its parameters, and the setup can be written to a folder and read back ([next section](#keeping-a-setup-freeze-save-to-a-folder-load)).
- A saved project reopens as it was: on Connect, the panel reads its layers' addresses back into its checkboxes and settings.
- Pure Python on QGIS's own API, no dependency (numpy, which QGIS ships, for the Python backend). QGIS is a flat, north-up client that picks the tiles it loads: the plugin never asks for 3D, tilted, rotated or split views, so the service it talks to needs no browser.

### Keeping a setup: Freeze, save to a folder, load

0.12.0. The question it answers: a setup seen as working must not be at the mercy of the next slider moved.

**What there was before.** The layers of **Terrain Viewer (live)** are bound to the panel: every change of a setting rewrites their addresses. They do survive in a saved project (on Connect the panel reads them back). **Freeze** already copied them into a dated group the panel never touches, but a copy said nothing of its settings beyond its address, and nothing could be written to a folder or read back.

**What 0.12.0 adds**, on the Freeze button and the menu on its arrow:

| Action | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Freeze** | As before, a dated group of plain copies beside the live set, which goes on being edited. Now each copy carries its parameters: as custom properties (`terrain_viewer/params`, JSON: mode, form, backend, source, every setting, opacity, blending, the light for a shaded mode; `terrain_viewer/link`, the app link of the whole setup; `terrain_viewer/frozen_at`), and as text in its abstract and its metadata, which QGIS shows when the layer is hovered in the layer tree and under **Layer Properties › Information**. The group carries the whole setup (`terrain_viewer/setup`) |
| **Save setup to folder...** | Asks for a folder and writes a new dated subfolder in it (`terrain-viewer-setup-2026-11-27-1405`; a second save the same minute gets `-2`: never over an earlier setup): `setup.json`, `layers.qlr` and, under the Python backend, `vectors.gpkg`. Nothing is added to the project |
| **Save setup and GeoTIFFs to folder...** | The same, plus `rasters/<mode>.tif` for each raster layer drawn |
| **Load setup...** | Reads a `setup.json` back into the panel |

The files:

- **`setup.json`**: `link`, the setup as a Terrain Viewer link (`https://terrain-viewer.iconem.com/?...`: the format the web app and **Open link** read, with only what differs from the app's defaults: the modes' switches, their settings and opacities, the source, the light, the basemap, the camera), the same as `query`, and `panel`, the plugin's own: the backend and its address, the source (a layer of the project or a file too, which a link cannot name), the light and its mode, the basemap, and for each mode on its forms (Color, Values, Vector), its opacity and the value of every setting, the defaults included. Never an API key.
- **`layers.qlr`**: a QGIS layer definition of the layers drawn, in their dated group, with their styles, opacity, blending and the parameters above. Drag it into any project, or **Layer › Add from Layer Definition File**. Its layers are plain: no panel needed to draw them, none will change them.
- **`vectors.gpkg`**: the Python backend's contour lines, iso-lines, graticule and plane slicer areas are memory layers computed for the view, which a layer definition cannot hold; they are written here and `layers.qlr` reads them from it.
- **`rasters/*.tif`**, when asked: each raster layer for **the map's extent at the map's pixel size** (the canvas's width and height, the longer side capped at 4096 px), in EPSG:3857, read through the layer's own QGIS provider, so **with every backend**: a Color form as an RGBA GeoTIFF of its tiles (the zoom QGIS draws, without the layer's opacity or blending), a Values form as Float32 with its numbers. Vector forms have no raster. `setup.json` lists them with their extent and size.

**Load setup** opens the setup's link as **Open link** does, then sets the plugin's own on top: each mode's forms and exact settings, the source, the light's mode. The backend is left as it is; when the setup was saved under another, the status line says so.

What a frozen layer is, and is not:

- **Its settings are frozen, its pixels are not.** A frozen layer and a `.qlr` layer still ask the backend that drew them for their tiles: a tile service at its address, or the Python backend's server on `127.0.0.1` (the same port each session; started when the panel opens with that backend). Without it they draw nothing. The GeoTIFFs are the pixels.
- **A keyed source's key is in the layers' addresses**, so in `layers.qlr` as in a saved project. Not in `setup.json`, nor in a copy's parameters.
- **Left out**: a `.qgz` project (the `.qlr` goes into any project), a choice of resolution for the GeoTIFFs, the Historical mode's dated layers (they are plain layers of the project already), and switching backend on load.

### The Vector library

0.12.0. The app's [vector overlays](/features/vector-overlays) library (buildings, heritage, land cover and land use, a roads and labels reference) as QGIS layers: a folded **Vector library** section with a select, grouped as in the app, and **Add**. An entry is added to a group **Vector overlays (library)** at the top of the layer tree, colored by its categories with its legend, carrying its attribution, licence and description (Layer Properties › Information; the layer's attribution). It is a layer of the project like any other: the panel does not change it afterwards, and it needs no backend.

What QGIS reads natively, measured in QGIS 4.2 and 3.44 (2026-11-27):

| Entry | In QGIS | Entries |
|---|---|---|
| A `{z}/{x}/{y}` vector tile template | A vector tile layer | IGN OCS GE cover and use |
| A TileJSON | A vector tile layer, on the tiles address the TileJSON names | OpenStreetMap buildings (OpenFreeMap) |
| A MapLibre style | A vector tile layer with the style imported by QGIS itself, cut to lines and labels | OpenStreetMap roads and labels (Liberty) |
| A GeoJSON | A vector layer (the file read whole by GDAL) | UNESCO World Heritage List and components |
| A PMTiles archive over https | **Not as a vector tile layer**: QGIS opens a PMTiles file on disk only. As a vector layer of GDAL's PMTiles driver over `/vsicurl/`, one per source layer, which picks the archive's zoom for the view | VIDA buildings; the four Heritage Watch AI maps |
| A PMTiles archive on a host GDAL cannot read | Overture's host answers HTTP 400 to the two requests GDAL sizes a file with (a `HEAD`, a `GET` without a range). The plugin's own server on `127.0.0.1` reads the archive by range requests (`live/pmtiles.py`, the reader of the building shadows) and hands it to QGIS as plain vector tiles (`/pmtiles/{z}/{x}/{y}.pbf?url=...`) | Overture buildings, land cover and land use |

- **Styled from the entry's categories**: a rule per category with its color and label (the first that matches, as in the app), then "other"; fills at 70 % with a thin outline, outlines only where the app draws outlines only, ringed dots for points. A vector layer gets a real QGIS legend; change it under Layer Properties › Symbology.
- **Overture's archives** are read from its current monthly release, found from its catalog as the app does, else the release the entry falls back to. Their layers draw while the panel is open: the server is a thread of QGIS, started with the entry and again when a project that holds such a layer is opened with the panel (their addresses are moved to its port when it changed). Every other entry stands alone, with or without the plugin.
- **A planet archive is not asked from a wide view**: a GDAL PMTiles layer is drawn from the zoom the app draws it from (zoom 11 for the buildings), by QGIS's scale-based visibility. Do not ask such a layer for its feature count or its attribute table: that reads the planet.
- **A GeoJSON the app serves itself** (UNESCO's lists) is read from the app's site, else from the `dataset-indexes` release the site's build takes it from (an entry newer than the last deploy: the components list, on 2026-11-27).
- **Measured** (`test_overlays_live.py`, 2026-11-27, QGIS 4.2 and 3.44, a 400 x 300 px picture at each entry's sample place): all 14 entries open and draw in their categories' colors; a layer opens in 0 to 2 s and draws in 0.1 to 1.1 s, VIDA's 2.7 billion footprints through GDAL in 3.6 s.
- **Left out**: the app's 3D extrusions and color by height, its feature cards, an entry's own MapLibre filter (the heritage inventories' companion shapes are drawn unfiltered), a source of your own (add it with QGIS's own dialogs), and reading a link's overlays in **Open link**.

The list is the plugin's own copy of the app's library (`assets/app_defaults.json`, written by `make_app_defaults.py` from `lib/custom-sources.json`): a new entry of the app reaches the panel with the next plugin release.

### Install

Two ways. The first is the one to prefer: QGIS then updates the plugin by itself.

1. **From the plugin repository**: give QGIS the address of the project's `plugins.xml` once, install both plugins from the Plugin Manager, and their updates arrive through QGIS as for any plugin ([the steps](#add-the-plugin-repository-to-qgis)).
2. **From a zip**: download the zip of a release and install it by hand ([below](#from-a-zip)); the panel's own Update button then keeps Terrain Viewer current, QGIS does not.

#### Add the plugin repository to QGIS

1. **Plugins › Manage and Install Plugins...**, then the **Settings** tab (the last one on the left).
2. Under **Plugin Repositories**, press **Add...**.
3. **Name**: `Terrain Viewer` (any name). **URL**:

   ```text
   https://github.com/Iconem/terrain-viewer/releases/download/qgis-plugins-repository/plugins.xml
   ```

   Leave **Authentication** empty and **Enabled** ticked, then **OK**. QGIS fetches the list at once.
4. Tick **Check for Updates on Startup**, at the top of the same tab, to be told when a new version is out.
5. Only for 0.11.0 and earlier, also tick **Show also Experimental Plugins** on that tab: QGIS 4 hides 0.11.0 without it. 0.12.0 no longer needs it ([why](#experimental-until-0110)).
6. On the **All** tab, search `Terrain Viewer`, check that its author is **Iconem**, and press **Install Plugin**; the same for **Send2MapApp**. In QGIS 3.40 to 3.x, read [this caution](#another-plugin-has-the-same-folder-name-on-pluginsqgisorg) first: plugins.qgis.org has an unrelated plugin with the same folder name.

From then on QGIS offers each new version under **Upgradeable** ([how often it looks](#how-the-repository-follows-releases)).

**Steps 1 to 3 as one line.** No link can do this: QGIS registers no `qgis:` address and no file type that adds a repository or installs a plugin ([what was checked](#a-button-that-opens-qgis-with-the-repository-added-there-is-none)). The nearest thing is one line for QGIS's Python console (**Plugins › Python Console**, paste, Enter), which adds the repository and fetches its list; then install from the **All** tab as in step 6:

```python
from qgis.core import QgsSettings; import pyplugin_installer; QgsSettings().setValue("app/plugin_repositories/Terrain Viewer/url", "https://github.com/Iconem/terrain-viewer/releases/download/qgis-plugins-repository/plugins.xml"); pyplugin_installer.instance().reloadAndExportData()
```

It writes the same setting the **Add...** dialog writes (`app/plugin_repositories/<name>/url`; a repository is enabled unless said otherwise) and asks QGIS's own installer to reload its repositories. Checked inside QGIS 4.2 and 3.44 (2026-11-27, a new profile each): the repository is listed, enabled and fetched, and Send2MapApp shows in the list; Terrain Viewer 0.11.0 shows in QGIS 4.2 once the experimental tick is on (until 0.12.0 is released), and in QGIS 3.44 the entry of that folder name was the other plugin's, 1.3.1. It installs nothing by itself, on purpose: installing by the plugin's id from the console took the other plugin of the same folder name in QGIS 3.44 ([the caution](#another-plugin-has-the-same-folder-name-on-pluginsqgisorg)).

##### A button that opens QGIS with the repository added: there is none

Asked for the docs' download button. What was checked (2026-11-27):

- **No URL scheme.** QGIS's sources register none on any platform: the Linux desktop entry runs `qgis %F` for file types only (no `x-scheme-handler`), the macOS `Info.plist` declares document types and no `CFBundleURLSchemes`, and nothing in the application handles an address handed over by the system. On this Windows machine the registry has no `qgis` protocol; the installer registers four file types (`.qgz` and `.qgs` QGIS Project, `.qlr` QGIS Layer Definition, `.qml` QGIS Layer Settings, `.qpt` a print template), each opened with `qgis.bat "%1"`.
- **What a file could do.** A `.qlr` adds layers and nothing else. A `.qgz` can carry Python macros that would run such a line when the project opens, but QGIS asks before running them (Settings › Options › General, "Enable macros": Ask by default), and a project that changes the plugin repositories of whoever opens it is not something to publish. A `.py` is opened by the system's Python, not by QGIS; `qgis --code file.py` runs one, from a command line only.
- **So**: the docs keep a download button and show the one line above, which a reader copies from its code block.

#### Updates

Two ways, both reading the project's GitHub releases (0.9.0), neither needing plugins.qgis.org nor an email:

- **A QGIS plugin repository**, added once ([the steps](#add-the-plugin-repository-to-qgis), the install route to prefer). QGIS then lists Terrain Viewer and Send2MapApp under **All**, and offers their new versions under **Upgradeable**, as for any repository. The file is a standard QGIS repository listing (`make_zip.py --plugins-xml <tag>`): QGIS keys a plugin by its `file_name` up to the first dot (`terrain_viewer.0.9.0.zip`, the plugin's folder name), and downloads `download_url`, the zip on its dated release. Each release workflow run writes it to its own release and copies it to the fixed release `qgis-plugins-repository` (created by the workflow the first time, `--prerelease --latest=false`), so the address never changes.
- **The panel.** A few seconds after it opens, at most once a day, the panel reads the same `plugins.xml` in the background. When it lists a newer Terrain Viewer, a line at the top says so with an **Update to x.y.z** button: the zip is downloaded through QGIS's network manager (its proxy and SSL settings), checked against the sha256 that `plugins.xml` carries (an element of ours, which QGIS ignores), unpacked over the plugin's folder (files the new version dropped are removed; a development `server/` stays), and the plugin is reloaded (QGIS's own `reloadPlugin`; restart QGIS if anything looks off). A development copy (a link into a checkout, a folder inside a git repository) is never overwritten: the line says to update it with git. The check is off with the QGIS setting `terrain_viewer/checkUpdates` set to `false`.

#### How the repository follows releases

The repository's address ([added once](#add-the-plugin-repository-to-qgis)) follows new releases by itself: it is a fixed release, `qgis-plugins-repository`, whose one file each run of the release workflow replaces with the new release's `plugins.xml` (`gh release upload qgis-plugins-repository plugins.xml --clobber` in `.github/workflows/qgis-plugins.yml`), which points at the new zips on their dated release. Nothing to change in QGIS after a release. A dated release's own `plugins.xml` (`.../releases/download/qgis-plugins-2026-11-26e/plugins.xml`) stays on that version for ever: use the fixed address.

**How often QGIS looks.** There is no interval to set in QGIS 3.44 or 4.2 (older versions had a list, from every start to once a month): with **Check for Updates on Startup** ticked, QGIS fetches the repositories at start when the last check is at least three days old (`CHECK_ON_START_INTERVAL = 3` in QGIS's `pyplugin_installer/installer_data.py`, the same in both versions), and tells in its main window when a plugin can be upgraded. Opening **Manage and Install Plugins** fetches them at any time, and **Reload Repository**, on the Settings tab, again. The panel's own check (below) runs once a day whatever these settings.

**When the Upgradeable tab shows.** Only while at least one installed plugin has a newer version in an enabled repository: QGIS hides the tab when the list would be empty, as it hides **New** and **Invalid** (and **Not installed** with no repository fetched). So yes: no plugin to upgrade, no tab. Checked inside QGIS 4.2 itself (2026-11-26, an empty profile): the tab is hidden; one installed plugin marked upgradeable and it appears; back, and it hides again. A plugin is upgradeable when the version a repository offers is greater than the installed one (QGIS compares the version numbers), counting experimental versions only with **Show also Experimental Plugins** ticked. For Terrain Viewer, three things can therefore keep the tab away while a newer version exists: the repository not added (or disabled), the list not fetched since the release (open the Plugin Manager, or **Reload Repository**), or, up to 0.11.0 only, **Show also Experimental Plugins** not ticked ([below](#experimental-until-0110)).

#### Experimental until 0.11.0

Up to 0.11.0, Terrain Viewer's `metadata.txt` said `experimental=True`, and QGIS leaves such a plugin out of its lists until **Show also Experimental Plugins** is ticked (Settings tab). Measured in a new profile with the repository added (2026-11-27): QGIS 4.2 does not list 0.11.0 at all without the tick, and lists it with it. **0.12.0 is `experimental=False`**: the Python backend is the default since 0.9.0, every mode is drawn by it, and three rounds of releases went through the update path; `plugins.xml` takes the flag from `metadata.txt` (`make_zip.py --plugins-xml`), so the first release of 0.12.0 lists it as a stable plugin and the tick is no longer needed.

- **On 0.11.0 or earlier, tick it** to see the plugin in QGIS 4: Settings tab, **Show also Experimental Plugins**. QGIS 3.44 and 4.2 keep it under `plugin-manager/allow-experimental` in the profile's ini (`[plugin-manager]`, `allow-experimental=true`); the key of older QGIS versions, `app/plugin_installer/allowExperimental`, is no longer read by either.
- **A user on 0.11.0 with the tick off** is offered 0.12.0 all the same once it is released: a stable version in a repository is always counted.

#### Another plugin has the same folder name on plugins.qgis.org

Found on 2026-11-27: plugins.qgis.org lists an unrelated plugin also named Terrain Viewer (by LiDARgeek, a 3D DEM viewer, version 1.3.1, for QGIS 3.40 to 3.99) whose folder is `terrain_viewer` too. QGIS keys a plugin by its folder name across every repository, so in **QGIS 3.40 to 3.x** with both repositories enabled the Plugin Manager shows one "Terrain Viewer" entry, with whichever repository's metadata it read last (`pyplugin_installer/installer_data.py`, `rebuild`), and can offer the other plugin as an upgrade of this one. In a new QGIS 3.44 profile with both repositories, the entry shown was the other plugin's. Installing by that id from the Python console (`installPlugin("terrain_viewer")`) installed the other plugin in QGIS 3.44. QGIS 4 is not concerned (that plugin stops at 3.99).

- **Check the author before installing or upgrading from the Plugin Manager in QGIS 3**: this plugin's is Iconem, its version 0.x.
- **The panel's own Update button is not concerned**: it reads this project's `plugins.xml` alone.
- In QGIS 3, if the list shows the other plugin, install from the zip (below) and update from the panel.

#### With the desktop app

The simplest local setup: run the [Terrain Viewer desktop app](/features/advanced/desktop#local-tile-service), turn on **Settings > Desktop: local tile service** (port 3400 by default), then in QGIS open the Terrain Viewer panel with the address `http://localhost:3400` (the default) and press **Connect**. The desktop app runs the same service the bundles carry, on this computer only, for as long as the app is open (and again at its next launch while the setting stays on). With another port, type `http://localhost:<port>` in the panel.

#### The packaged tile server, installed from the panel

The **Packaged tile server** backend, picked at the top of the panel: a line saying what is installed, an **Install** (or **Remove**) button and **Connect**. Connect installs it the first time, then starts it.

- **Install (48 MB)** downloads the bundle of this platform (Windows x64, macOS arm64 or x64, Linux x64 or arm64) from the GitHub release named in the plugin's `service_pins.json`, checks its sha256 and size against the pin (a different file is refused and nothing is installed), and unpacks it into the plugin's data folder in the QGIS profile, `<profile>/terrain_viewer/tile-service/<release>/<platform>/`. The download goes through QGIS's network manager (its proxy and SSL settings), in a QGIS task with its progress in the status line; GitHub's redirect to its download host is followed. A plugin version pins one release of the service, so the plugin and the service it starts are always a tested pair; a new pin installs next to the old one, which is then deleted. **Remove** stops and deletes it.
- It runs only when asked for: picking **Packaged tile server** (or **Connect** with it picked) starts it; the panel opening (the toolbar button, a saved project) never does, the status line then says to press Connect. Picking another backend stops it.
- **Connect** starts it as a subprocess, `bin/node server.mjs` in that folder, listening on this machine only (`127.0.0.1`), on port 3410 or, when that is taken, a free one; the address field shows it and the status line says where it runs and its process id. The first start takes about 3 s. A saved project's layers follow the port: on Connect their addresses are rewritten with the current one.
- It stops when the plugin unloads (QGIS quitting, the plugin disabled, reinstalled or updated) or another backend is picked; and, if QGIS goes without stopping it (a crash), by itself: it is started with `TV_EXIT_WITH_STDIN=1` and stops when its standard input, a pipe from QGIS, closes. Its log is `terrain-viewer-qgis-tile-service.log` in the system's temporary folder; the status line names it when the service does not start.
- **No Earthdata token in the plugin** (0.6.0; 0.5.0 had a field): a NASA Earthdata token for G-LiHT's protected files lives in a tile service's environment only (`EARTHDATA_TOKEN`). The service started from the panel inherits QGIS's environment, so an `EARTHDATA_TOKEN` set for QGIS reaches it; the plugin never stores one (0.6.0 deletes the old `terrain_viewer/earthdataToken` setting), no token goes into a layer's address or ships in a bundle, and the service reads no `.env` (`TV_ENV_FILE` points at nothing). The service's Earthdata proxy is unchanged.
- **No browser engine**: the service draws what QGIS asks for, flat north-up tiles, with the direct engine (Node and sharp). playwright-core is not in the bundle and the service runs with `TV_NO_BROWSER=1`, so it never looks for Chrome or Edge; a 3D, tilted, rotated or split view, or a source only the app draws, answers 501 with a message.
- If it stops while connected (a crash, the process killed), the background health check warns, and Connect starts it again.
- Zip extraction drops the executable bits on macOS and Linux: the plugin sets them on `bin/node` before starting it. The macOS Node is the one nodejs.org signs and notarizes; sharp's libraries come from its npm packages.
- A tile service unpacked as `terrain_viewer/server/` inside the plugin folder (a 0.4 platform zip, a developer's build with `make_zip.py --server-dir`) is used first, and the button is hidden.

#### How QGIS plugins ship native programs

What the plugin downloads is a Node runtime (the official nodejs.org build, checked against its `SHASUMS256.txt` when the bundle is made), the tile service's JavaScript (`tile-server/*.mjs` and the app's code bundled into `generated/`), and its production node_modules, among them sharp's prebuilt native libraries (libvips) for that platform. It then runs it as a local HTTP service on `127.0.0.1`, which QGIS reads as XYZ and GDAL layers like any web service. Other plugins that need native code have done it three ways:

- **Download on demand.** The classic [WhiteboxTools for QGIS](https://plugins.qgis.org/plugins/wbt_for_qgis) plugin does not include the WhiteboxTools binary: the user downloads it from [whiteboxgeo.com](https://www.whiteboxgeo.com/download-whiteboxtools) or the project's GitHub releases and sets its path in **Settings > Options > Processing > Providers > WhiteboxTools** ([its manual](https://jblindsay.github.io/wbt_book/qgis_plugin.html)); the `whitebox` packages for Python and R download it themselves on first use. Terrain Viewer's **Install** is the second form inside QGIS: one click, a pinned version and a checked hash instead of a manual download and a path to type.
- **Installed with QGIS, the plugin only pointing at it.** GRASS ships with QGIS's Windows and macOS installers and OSGeo4W (a separate package on Linux), and its Processing provider is part of QGIS. SAGA has been a separate plugin since QGIS 3.30 ([Processing Saga NextGen Provider](https://plugins.qgis.org/plugins/processing_saga_nextgen/)): SAGA itself comes from OSGeo4W or SAGA's own downloads, and the plugin is told its folder. The Orfeo ToolBox provider likewise needs OTB installed apart and its folder set in the Processing options. None of these plugins carries the program.
- **Bundled in the plugin.** Rare, because the zip becomes one per platform and large, and a binary in a plugin cannot be reviewed as source. Terrain Viewer 0.3 and 0.4 did it (48 to 60 MB zips, one per platform); 0.5 moved to the download.

What is less common is the form: a local HTTP service rather than a command-line program called per run. It suits tiles, which QGIS already knows how to ask for, cache and cancel; a Processing provider over the same service (one algorithm per mode, writing a GeoTIFF) would be the WhiteboxTools-like form for batch work.

`--service-bundle` needs the root install and tile-server's (`tile-server/build.mjs` bundles the app's code into `generated/` first). It takes Node 22.22.3 from nodejs.org, checked against its `SHASUMS256.txt`, and installs tile-server's production dependencies flat (`node-linker=hoisted`: a zip keeps no symlinks) with pnpm's `supportedArchitectures` set to the platform, so the right `@img/sharp-*` binaries land; source maps, type declarations and playwright-core are left out. The [QGIS plugins workflow](https://github.com/Iconem/terrain-viewer/actions/workflows/qgis-plugins.yml) (`.github/workflows/qgis-plugins.yml`, on demand) builds each platform's bundle on its own runner and starts it there with `smoke_bundled.py`; with `release_tag` it attaches the bundles, writes their hashes into `service_pins.json`, builds the plain zips with those pins inside and attaches them too, with `service_pins.json`, `SHA256SUMS` and `plugins.xml` (also copied to the fixed release `qgis-plugins-repository`, [updates](#updates)). The pins file is then committed back, so the repository's plugin pins the released bundles. A release: create it (`gh release create qgis-plugins-<date> --prerelease --latest=false`), run the workflow with its tag, commit the attached `service_pins.json`.

## Send2MapApp: "Open in"

A second plugin in the same folder, [`qgis-plugin/send2map_app/`](https://github.com/Iconem/terrain-viewer/tree/main/qgis-plugin/send2map_app): the place shown or clicked in QGIS opened in another viewer at the same place and scale, from a dropdown on the Web toolbar (the current view, in the destination picked, which is remembered), the button itself (a map tool: the point clicked), the map canvas's right-click menu ("Open in" at the point clicked) or **Web > Send2MapApp**. QGIS 3.22 to 4.x; install it from the [`qgis-plugins-2026-11-25c`](https://github.com/Iconem/terrain-viewer/releases/tag/qgis-plugins-2026-11-25c) release as above.

- **A QGIS map tool**: the button is checked while the tool is on, with a crosshair cursor carrying an "open elsewhere" badge, and unchecks itself when another map tool (Pan, Identify, ...) is picked, as QGIS's own tools do. The destination picked is kept in QGIS's settings (`plugins/send2map_app/destination`).
- **Icons**: QGIS-style SVGs for the plugin, Terrain Viewer and the web viewers, QGIS's own 3D globe for Google Maps 3D and Bing Maps 3D, the publishers' logos for Google Earth, Esri, Maxar and Search-EO.

- **Destinations**: every entry of the app's own "Open in" list (Iconem River-REM, Iconem Search-EO, ESRI Wayback, Google Maps 3D, Google Earth historical and 3D on the web, Bing Maps 3D, ShadeMap, LiDAR Lens, BBBike MapCompare), plus Terrain Viewer itself and its historical mode (historical-satellite.iconem.com), Maxar, and Google Earth Pro on the desktop through a KML file. LiDAR Lens is greyed out outside Britain and Ireland, as in the app.
- **The camera**: the canvas centre or the point clicked, the zoom whose 512 px tiles have the canvas's ground resolution there (the app's zoom, so the other viewer opens at the same scale in any project CRS), the heading from the canvas rotation.
- **One list**: the app's destinations are a Python port of `OPEN_IN_DESTINATIONS` (`components/TerrainControlPanel/open-in-links.tsx`). `python qgis-plugin/test_open_in.py` evaluates the TypeScript builders under Node and compares ids, labels, coverage and URLs for a set of cameras, so a destination added to the app fails the test until the plugin has it.
- **Lineage and licence**: it began as [Send2GE (`send2google_earth`)](https://plugins.qgis.org/plugins/send2google_earth/) by Maxim Dubinin and NextGIS ([source](https://github.com/nextgis/qgis_send2google_earth)), a map tool that sends the point clicked to Google Earth through a KML file. Iconem's additions in 2025 (as Send2MapApp: web destinations in a toolbar dropdown) and 2026 (the app's whole list, the right-click menu, the current view, QGIS 4) keep its licence, the GNU GPL v2 or later, and its copyright lines; that is why it is a plugin of its own rather than part of the MIT `terrain_viewer`.

## Design history

Before the plugins, QGIS got the app's modes only through the tile service's `/qgis.qgz`: a project with every mode as an XYZ layer, configured as a view was. A layer's settings lived in its address, and QGIS has no control bound to an XYZ address's parameters, so changing a ramp or a radius meant editing the layer's source URL. A plugin was the way to have controls. The plan (2026-11-23) had two steps, the first cheap:

1. **A panel that writes tile service layers**: written on 2026-11-23; Freeze came in 0.2.0, the "Terrain Viewer (live)" group in 0.2.1, and 0.2.2, the first release (2026-11-24), requires `/processes`. 0.3.0 (2026-11-25) shipped the tile service inside four platform zips of 48 to 60 MB, 0.4.0 made it off by default and dropped the browser engine, and 0.5.0 (the same day) went back to one plain 50 KB zip with the service downloaded by the **Install** button.
2. **The modes computed in QGIS**, with no tile service: the experimental local compute (0.5.0, 16 modes since 0.6.0), below.

### Step 2 as planned

The plan for step 2, kept for reference; what was built and measured is on [QGIS Live Compute](/dev/qgis-live-compute).

- **Reading:** GDAL reads the tiles and nothing else. A Terrarium or Terrain-RGB pyramid through GDAL's WMS/TMS driver (the XML of `gdalTileSource` in `lib/terrain-tile-spec.ts`), a COG or VRT directly, a raw WMS through GDAL's WMS driver. Decoding the RGB packing into metres is numpy (the formulas of `lib/elevation-encoding.ts`). The sources only the app's protocols read (LERC, quantized mesh, GEM-15's cells, a difference) need their own reader or stay on step 1.
- **Computing:** numpy, not `gdaldem`: the app's modes are its own (LRM, sky view factor, openness, matcap, Phong, the mound detector, the eigen-based ones), and the point is that QGIS shows what the app shows. Each `lib/*-protocol.ts` is a kernel over an elevation grid with a margin, which ports to numpy array code: slope, aspect, curvature, TPI, TRI and roughness in a few lines each; sky view factor and openness as the app's ray sweeps (vectorised per direction); matcap and Phong as the shaders' formulas per pixel; the color ramps from `lib/color-ramps.ts`.
- **Delivery:** a QGIS raster layer per mode, computed for the canvas extent at the canvas resolution and recomputed on pan and zoom (debounced, in a background task), or written to a GeoTIFF on request. Optionally a Processing provider, so each mode is also an algorithm for models and batch runs.
- **Checking:** the port is checked against the app tile by tile, as `tile-server/compare-engines.mjs` checks the direct engine against the browser: same tiles, a table of differences.
- **Keeping in step:** two implementations of each mode drift. The comparison above runs in CI on a fixed set of tiles, so a change to a protocol that the Python port does not follow fails a check.

Where it stands (0.7.0: the Python backend is one of the panel's three, under the same modes tree): reading through GDAL, the numpy kernels, delivery as Float32 values GDAL reads (so QGIS's own color ramps apply) or as colored tiles, and the tile-by-tile check are done for 16 modes, Phong and matcap included, plus contour lines as a vector layer per view (`qgis-plugin/test_live.py`, against the app's kernels in Node, the tile service and gdaldem). Not done: the comparison in CI, the Processing provider, and the modes that need a whole view (the mound detector, local dominance, the eigen-based ones); for those, the tile service layers stay the way in.
